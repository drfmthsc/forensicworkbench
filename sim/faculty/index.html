The design decisions, and the reasoning behind them. If a delegate asks why we did it this way, the answer is here.

Why two encounters

Because the second one is where the room actually learns.

The first encounter is raw. Twenty-eight people are watching a colleague do something difficult. They are partly watching the person rather than the behaviour, and nobody has handed them the vocabulary yet. They see it, but they do not see it clearly.

Then they spend sixteen minutes taking it apart. By the time the second learner walks in, the room has been told what to look for, and has just practised naming it out loud at their tables. They watch the second encounter with different eyes. That change, between the first and the second, is the thing we are teaching.

Three other things it buys

  • The second learner has watched the first. So the debrief question becomes: you saw what happened last time, what did you do differently, and what happened when you did? That is a far better question than anything you can ask someone going in cold.
  • It shows that one variable changes everything. Same case. Same actor. Same room. She withholds consent for one part. The delegates see with their own eyes that a scenario is a machine with adjustable parts. That is what makes them think they could build one, which is the point of the design bench.
  • It shows the debrief twice, run by two people, with the same structure. The room concludes that debriefing is a technique, not a personality. Show it once and they will conclude you are just good at it.

The cost is real and worth saying out loud. Only two of thirty sit in the chair. And the second encounter is thirteen minutes that could have gone somewhere else.

If you ever have to drop one, drop the second encounter and keep both debriefs on the first. Never two encounters and one debrief. The scenario is not the intervention.

Why a fishbowl instead of stations

At four hours you rotate twenty people through four stations, and everyone sits in the chair. At two hours that same architecture gives you groups of seven and thirty-minute rounds. Too short for an encounter, a debrief and a meta-debrief.

So we run one case in one room and everyone watches. Two people take the chair. The compensation is that thirty people watch a debrief being run properly, twice. For a faculty development purpose, that is worth more than thirty people running a simulation badly once.

Why every table has a job

Twenty-eight people watching with nothing to do is an audience. Twenty-eight people watching with one specific thing to look for is a faculty.

Tables E and F are the two that change the temperature of a large room. E watches only Meera, which stops the audience grading the doctor and starts them seeing the case. F watches as defence counsel, which recovers part of the courtroom station we had to drop, and usually produces the sharpest single observation of the day.

Why the debrief gets more minutes than the scenario

Thirty-eight minutes of discussion against thirty-two of scenario. Drawn to scale on the first slide, so nobody misses it.

The scenario only creates something to discuss. Across twenty years of reviews, the feature most consistently linked to learning is feedback and debriefing. Run a scenario and send people home and you have done a quarter of the teaching and generated all of the anxiety.

Why nothing is scored

Teaching and examining are different jobs. Mixing them damages both.

The moment people believe they are being marked, they perform instead of learning. Nothing today is scored, nothing is reported to anyone's head of department, and we say so in the opening. That is not softness. It is the condition that makes the rest work.

Why the examination is narrated, never performed

A conference hall of thirty people watching a colleague be physically examined in a sexual assault scenario is the one thing here that could genuinely go wrong in public. It is also entirely avoidable.

Narrating loses nothing we are trying to teach. The sequence, the consent, the explanation before each step, the prohibited practices: all of it is testable out loud. What we would gain by touching a person is realism we do not need and cannot justify.

The case, fixed

Meera Kandpal, 23. Works in a tailoring unit in Dehradun. Hindi-speaking. Brought to casualty at 14:40 by a woman police constable with a written requisition under Sections 63/64 BNS. Incident reported at about 19:30 the previous evening, so roughly nineteen hours ago. Her elder sister is waiting outside.

What she holds back until the learner earns it: the assailant is known to her, the brother of a co-worker, and she had gone to collect a payment. She bathed and changed, and brought the salwar in a plastic carry bag because her sister told her to. Abrasion on the left forearm, tenderness over the right cheek. Last period nineteen days ago, no contraception. She has already described the incident twice, at the police station and to a male doctor who sent her on. She is frightened her employer will find out.

Why each fact is in there

  • Nineteen hours. Inside the twenty-four-hour requirement of Section 184 BNSS and well inside the evidence window, so "too late" is not available as an escape.
  • Bathed and changed. Forces the learner to manage their own "there will be no evidence" anxiety without transmitting it to her.
  • Known assailant, gone to collect a payment. Closes off the stranger-in-the-dark script, and hands the blaming question its opening. Watch how fast someone reaches for it.
  • Two previous narrations. Sets up the single most testable behaviour in the room: does he make her tell it a third time from the beginning.
  • Nineteen days since her period. Mid-cycle, so emergency contraception is genuinely indicated. If the learner never offers it, that is a real omission and not a trick.

Room state at the start

  • Two chairs at forty-five degrees, the one nearer the door is hers. Screen. Couch or table standing in for one. Pelvic model on a side table, draped.
  • Door frame or taped line for the constable's entrances.
  • On the table: MoHFW proforma, layered consent page, interim note template, sterile swabs, paper envelopes, labels, seal.
  • Tissues, water and a shawl within the learner's reach. Offering them is scoreable.
  • Learner has read the case card outside and said one sentence aloud about their first move. Nothing else.

What the dry run found

The scenario was run against the clock before it was written up. Nine things broke. Seven are fixed in the cue list above. Two need the five of us to decide.

1. A pile-up at minutes three and four. Fixed.

Two cues landed sixty seconds apart, both while the learner was still taking consent. Nobody could handle both. The moment stopped testing anything. The constable's line moves to minute two. Hers moves to minute five.

2. The constable's last cue never fired in Encounter 2. Fixed.

Her question about the report sat at minute fourteen. The second encounter freezes at thirteen. Encounter 2 now has its own cue times instead of borrowing the first one's.

3. The changed variable arrived too late to change anything. Fixed.

Her refusal only works if the learner has got as far as proposing an examination. In the dry run, thirteen minutes went on consent and history. The refusal never came up. Encounter 2 was just a shorter Encounter 1. She now volunteers it herself at minute four. That is what makes the second encounter a different problem.

4. Withdrawal could kill fourteen minutes in front of thirty people. Fixed, with a limit.

Trigger withdrawal at minute three and the rule keeps her closed for the rest of the encounter. In a teaching room that is right. In a conference hall it is fourteen minutes of nothing. So Dr Raju may cue one half-step back up. Once only. And only if the learner names what he did and apologises. If he doesn't, Dr Chhabra freezes at minute twelve and debriefs the withdrawal. That is a better debrief than watching the clock run out.

5. Nobody owned the moment the learner walks toward her. Fixed.

The absolute rule has to be enforced in under two seconds. The run sheet never said by whom. It is Dr Raju. The line is: Doctor, describe it to us instead. Said flat, without stopping the encounter.

6. Two minutes of writing produced nothing worth cross-examining. Fixed.

The dry-run learner spent the writing window deciding on a layout. There is now a printed template with five prompts. The two minutes go on content, and Table D gets something real to attack.

7. Consent was being signed after the fiction had ended. Fixed.

The earlier version had the consent page completed at the freeze. That is backwards. Consent is taken during the consultation. He now ticks each layer as he takes it, and signs at the freeze.

8. Seventeen minutes may be too long for Beat 3. Needs a decision.

Narrating the examination took about four minutes, not five. The last minute drifted. Two options. Accept a loose ending, or cut Encounter 1 to sixteen and give the minute to the harvest. My view: leave it at seventeen. A learner running out of things to say is worth debriefing. Dr Chhabra decides.

9. The sister is written in but never used. Needs a decision.

Inviting her in is a scoreable act of support. It also shows the difference between a chaperone and a person she chose herself. But it needs a third volunteer and a chair. At a conference that is one more thing to go wrong. Staff her properly, or cut her from the case card. Half-staffing is the worst option. The learner asks for her, and nobody appears.

Interim note template

Half a page, printed, handed over at the freeze. Five prompts, nothing more.

1. Time requisition received. Time examination began.
2. Consent: examination, samples, information to police. Each one agreed or declined, with the time.
3. What she said, in her own words, in no more than three lines.

What we are actually running

0 of 10 ticked
1. Simulation is a technique, not equipment

The instinct is to think this needs a skills lab and a mannequin. It doesn't. You need four things. A case. Someone to play the patient. A room. And someone who can run the discussion afterwards.

What it does is narrow. It moves the first attempt. Every one of us examined our first survivor on a real survivor. Alone. At an odd hour. Nobody watching. A student can score eighty per cent on sexual offences and still ask a survivor why she went there. A written paper cannot catch that. This can.

Our whole session costs under a thousand rupees. That fact is part of what we are teaching.

2. The prebrief, and why it is not a formality

Three things make a room safe enough for someone to be bad at something in front of thirty people.

  • The fiction contract. We agree to treat something artificial as real, and we agree that when the facilitator says freeze, it stops being real. Both halves matter, and the second half is the one people forget.
  • The basic assumption, said out loud: everyone here is intelligent, well trained, cares about doing well, and wants to improve. Say it every time, even when you doubt it.
  • Confidentiality. Nothing is scored, nothing is reported, nothing leaves the room.

Skip it and two things follow. Defensive performance in the scenario. Silence in the discussion. People then decide simulation doesn't work. What failed was three minutes of framing.

3. The freeze

One word, said flatly. Not "okay, I think we can stop there", not "shall we leave it?". Just "Freeze."

The flatness is the point. The room sees the pretend thing end on command. Then she stands, leaves the chair, and sits with us. That movement closes the fiction better than any announcement.

Call it on the clock, not when the scenario feels finished. Real consultations get interrupted. A learner who managed only consent and half a history in seventeen minutes has given you plenty to discuss.

4. Not interrupting, which is the hardest habit to break

You are a teacher. Someone does it wrong in front of you. Every instinct says correct it now. Here, that instinct is the enemy.

If the learner narrates a two-finger test, let it finish. Interrupt, and thirty people learn that you will step in and rescue them. Every performance after that goes cautious and false. You also lose the most useful thing in the room. An error you can examine with the person who made it.

The exception is never about content. Stop only for three things. Someone is being harmed. The absolute rule is about to be broken. It has turned into comedy.

5. Running a standardised patient

A standardised patient is not a volunteer who improvises. She works to a written brief. The brief is what makes her responses teach anything.

  • She responds to behaviour. Our Meera moves between closed, opening and disclosing depending on what the doctor does. That calibration is the engine. Without it the encounter is just a scene.
  • Named withdrawal triggers. Specific things, agreed in advance, that make her shut down. Asking her to repeat the account. Any question about why she went there. Touching her without asking.
  • No invention. Anything outside the brief gets "mujhe yaad nahi". A detail invented in front of thirty people cannot be quietly corrected.
  • Out of role at the freeze, physically and immediately.

Budget 45 minutes to brief her, a week ahead. This is the item people cut when they are busy. It is also the item that decides whether the session works.

6. Observing: behaviour, not opinion

An observation you can act on has words and a time in it. An impression has neither.

He was insensitive. Nobody can discuss that. The learner can only agree or defend himself. He asked why she went there alone at minute six, and she stopped looking at him for four minutes. Everybody can examine that, including the man who said it.

The test: could someone disagree with you about whether it happened? If yes, it is opinion.

Try the six below. Most people get two wrong.

7. The four phases of a debrief

This is where the learning happens, so it gets more minutes than the scenario. The order is not arbitrary.

  • Reactions, two minutes. One word each. You are letting the emotion out of the room before anyone tries to think. Do not analyse, do not reassure.
  • Description, two minutes. Ask a table, not the learner, what happened in sequence. This agrees the facts and it protects the learner from opening with an attack on themselves.
  • Analysis, the bulk. Two points, not twenty. This is where advocacy-inquiry lives.
  • Application, three minutes. One thing each person will do differently. Cut this and nothing changes on Monday.

One thing to remember. Never open with: so, how do you think that went? A shaken learner will attack himself. The room then spends its energy consoling him instead of learning.

8. Advocacy and inquiry, the move to practise

Three parts, in one breath. What you saw. What worries you about it. Then a real question.

You never told her she could stop. That worries me. Consent becomes a signature, not a standing permission. What were you thinking at the time?

It is honest, because your view is on the table instead of hidden. It is still curious, because you might be wrong about why he did it. And it gives him something specific to answer.

Build one yourself. Take any moment from your own teaching this week.

9. The four ways a debrief is lost

Each of these feels reasonable at the time, which is why they are common.

  • The opener that invites self-attack. "How do you think that went?"
  • Hidden judgement. You have an opinion, you conceal it, and you ask leading questions until they guess it. This is sometimes taught as being non-judgemental. The room can always tell, and it feels like an interrogation.
  • Lecturing. If you are speaking for more than thirty seconds at a stretch, you have stopped debriefing.
  • Chasing every error. Pick two. Leave the rest. A debrief that covers everything changes nothing.

Four things a facilitator might say. One of them is the one to copy.

10. Time, cutting, and looking after the room

Cutting. Six tables at ninety seconds is nine minutes. Open the floor instead and three confident people will take twenty. Cut every table at the timer, including the professor, kindly and without apologising. The first time the room sees you cut someone senior, everyone relaxes, because the rule is visibly real.

Safeguarding. In a room of thirty, the statistics make it near certain that someone present has lived some version of this case. So: the subject goes in the programme in plain words, anyone may leave without explaining, one faculty member has no job except the room, and nobody who steps out is ever asked about it afterwards.

When you are behind. Cut the design bench first, the second encounter second. Never the discussion, and never the prebrief.

Before you run it for real

Rehearse the whole thing once, the five of us, with one of us playing the learner. Forty-five minutes. It is the only way to find out that the microphone doesn't reach the back row. Or that a cue you wrote for yourself makes no sense at speed.

Where this way of teaching came from, what to watch, and what to read if you want to go further.

Nine things worth knowing

Aviation got there about sixty years before medicine

Edwin Link built his Blue Box flight trainer in 1929, from bellows and valves out of his family's pipe organ business. Pilots were practising emergencies on the ground. Doctors were still learning theirs on patients. The debt goes past equipment. The word debriefing came to us from aviation, and so did most of what we teach about teams.

The most kissed face in the world belonged to a drowned woman

Resusci Anne arrived in 1960, made by a Norwegian toymaker, Åsmund Lærdal. Her face came from a death mask. An unidentified young woman pulled from the Seine in the 1880s, known as L'Inconnue de la Seine. Lærdal had seen a copy of it hanging in a relative's house. He picked a woman's face partly because he thought male trainees would not want to practise mouth-to-mouth on a man. About 300 million people have trained on her since.

The first computer-controlled patient was built in 1967, then ignored for twenty years

Sim One breathed, blinked, had a pulse and responded to drugs. Exactly one was made. Computing was far too expensive and the idea found no market. High-fidelity mannequins did not come back until the late 1980s. Technology rarely fails on its merits.

Harvey has been teaching cardiology since 1968

The cardiology simulator built by Michael Gordon at Miami is still in use, fifty years on. Nothing else in our field has that record. Worth asking why a limited, focused, mechanical thing outlasted so much cleverer equipment.

Standardised patients were first dismissed as showbusiness

In the early 1960s a neurologist, Howard Barrows, trained a healthy person to play a patient so every student met the same case. Colleagues mocked it as a Hollywood technique. It became the OSCE, which is how most of us were examined.

France ran a national simulation programme in the 1750s

Angélique du Coudray, a Paris midwife, built a life-size birthing model from cloth, leather and real pelvic bones. She toured France for decades with royal backing, teaching village midwives to deliver safely. Thousands trained on it. National-scale simulation training, three hundred years ago, with no electricity.

Sushruta had students practise on vegetables first

The Sushruta Samhita says what to rehearse on before touching a patient. Incisions on gourds and melons. Punctures on a leather bag filled with water. Scraping on hides. What we are teaching at this workshop is not new to India. It is about as old as surgery.

The single most quoted line in the field is a warning about equipment

David Gaba's line is that simulation is a technique, not a technology. Quoted constantly. Ignored almost as often, usually by places that buy a mannequin and then wonder why nothing changed.

The evidence points at the conversation, not the kit

Across twenty years of reviews, one feature keeps coming out on top. Feedback and debriefing. Choose between a better mannequin and a better debrief, and the literature says buy the debrief. Our session costs under a thousand rupees. Same point, made with money.

Building a psychologically safe workplace Amy Edmondson, TEDxHGSE · 11 min The research behind the basic assumption. Her three moves are exactly what the prebrief does. Read: the evidence, in order of usefulness

  • Eppich and Cheng, Promoting Excellence and Reflective Learning in Simulation (PEARLS), Simulation in Healthcare, 2015. The structure of our debrief.
  • Rudolph, Raemer and Simon, Establishing a safe container for learning in simulation, Simulation in Healthcare, 2014. The prebrief, and why it is a standard rather than a courtesy.
  • Cheng and colleagues, Debriefing for technology-enhanced simulation: a systematic review and meta-analysis, Medical Education, 2014. The evidence that the conversation carries the effect.
  • Issenberg and colleagues, Features and uses of high-fidelity medical simulations that lead to effective learning, BEME review, Medical Teacher, 2005. Still the best map of what actually matters.
  • Cook and colleagues, Technology-enhanced simulation for health professions education, JAMA, 2011. The big meta-analysis to quote when someone asks whether it works.
  • Norman, Dror and Reznick, The minimal relationship between simulation fidelity and transfer of learning, Medical Education, 2012. The argument against buying expensive things.
  • Miller, The assessment of clinical skills, competence, performance, Academic Medicine, 1990. Four pages, and the pyramid in slide 2.
  • INACSL, Healthcare Simulation Standards of Best Practice, 2021. What good practice looks like, written down.

Read: the clinical and legal side

  • Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, Guidelines and Protocols: Medico-legal Care for Survivors and Victims of Sexual Violence, 2014. The proforma, the consent structure and the sampling sequence our case follows.
  • World Health Organization, Clinical Management of Rape and Intimate Partner Violence Survivors, revised 2019.
  • State of Jharkhand v. Shailendra Kumar Rai, Supreme Court of India, 2022, on the two-finger test. Short, readable, and worth reading in the original rather than in summary.
  • Lillu v. State of Haryana, (2013) 14 SCC 643.

Their pre-workshop reading

Sent out a week ahead. It carries the safeguarding note, the running order, four tappable infographics, and three exercises.

  • Behaviour or opinion. Six statements to sort. This trains the exact skill they need at their table.
  • How a discussion opens. Four openers to choose between. The trap is "so, how do you think that went".
  • A four-question self-check on cost, on which part carries the learning, on not interrupting, and on not marking.

Their six table jobs

They pick their letter and get that list. Each item carries a Proper and a Not proper button and its own remarks box, with a button to timestamp a moment and a free notes field underneath. They are told that leaving both unmarked means it never came up, which is not the same as not proper. At the end they can download the filled sheet as a spreadsheet, or copy it as text.

Printed sheets exist as well, for anyone who would rather write by hand or whose phone dies. Same items, same three columns.
