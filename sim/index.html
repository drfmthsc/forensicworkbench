Simulation, in plain words

Simulation is a way of teaching, not a piece of equipment. You rebuild a real situation closely enough to act in it. Someone makes a decision and sees what follows. Nobody real is harmed by a wrong move.

It does one thing. It moves the first attempt. Every one of us examined our first survivor on a real survivor. Alone. At an odd hour. Nobody watching.

Three questions that always come up

She refuses the internal examination. What now?

Record the refusal in her own words, dated and timed. Record what you explained before she refused. Carry on with everything she has agreed to. In the report, write: local examination not conducted, consent declined after explanation. Nothing more. Refusal is not a finding. It is not evidence of anything. Treatment, prophylaxis and follow-up carry on unchanged.

There are no injuries at all. What goes in the report?

No external injury was seen on the body, including the genital and perianal region, on examination at the stated time and interval after the reported incident. Then add the sentence that matters. Absence of injury neither confirms nor excludes sexual assault. Most genuine assaults leave no injury. Compliance under fear is not resistance, and mucosa heals fast.

The investigating officer asks whether it looks like rape.

You cannot answer it. Rape is a legal finding, not a medical one. You record what you found and what you collected. The court decides the rest. Say it kindly. The officer is not being improper. He is being hurried.

The law, in one screen

Section 184 BNSS 2023, formerly 164A CrPC. Medical examination of a victim of rape. A registered medical practitioner, in a government or local authority hospital where available, with consent, and she is to be sent within twenty-four hours of the information being received.

Section 48 BSA 2023, formerly 53A IEA. Character and previous sexual experience are not relevant to consent. So a question about past sexual history is not merely unkind. It gathers something the court is forbidden to use.

Section 120 BSA 2023, formerly 114A IEA. Where intercourse is proved and she states in evidence that she did not consent, the Court shall presume she did not. Consent is not a medical finding, so write nothing that invites speculation about it.

Section 397 BNSS and Section 200 BNS. Free first aid and treatment, and refusal to treat is punishable. Treatment is not conditional on an FIR, a requisition or a police escort.

No two-finger test. State of Jharkhand v. Shailendra Kumar Rai (2022): no scientific basis, re-traumatising, and conducting it amounts to misconduct. Record no opinion on whether the woman is habituated to sexual intercourse.

Nine things worth knowing

Aviation got there about sixty years before medicine

Edwin Link built his Blue Box flight trainer in 1929, from bellows and valves out of his family's pipe organ business. Pilots were practising emergencies on the ground. Doctors were still learning theirs on patients. The word debriefing came to us from aviation too.

The most kissed face in the world belonged to a drowned woman

Resusci Anne arrived in 1960, made by a Norwegian toymaker, Åsmund Lærdal. Her face came from a death mask. An unidentified young woman pulled from the Seine in the 1880s. Lærdal had seen a copy hanging in a relative's house. He picked a woman's face partly because he thought male trainees would not want to practise mouth-to-mouth on a man. About 300 million people have trained on her since.

The first computer-controlled patient was built in 1967, then ignored for twenty years

Sim One breathed, blinked, had a pulse and responded to drugs. Exactly one was made. Computing was far too expensive and the idea found no market. High-fidelity mannequins did not come back until the late 1980s.

Harvey has been teaching cardiology since 1968

The cardiology simulator built at Miami is still in use, fifty years on. Worth asking why a limited, focused, mechanical thing outlasted so much cleverer equipment.

Standardised patients were first dismissed as showbusiness

In the early 1960s a neurologist trained a healthy person to play a patient, so every student met the same case. Colleagues mocked it as a Hollywood technique. It became the OSCE, which is how most of us were examined.

France ran a national simulation programme in the 1750s

Angélique du Coudray, a Paris midwife, built a life-size birthing model from cloth, leather and real pelvic bones. She toured France for decades with royal backing, teaching village midwives to deliver safely. Thousands trained on it. No electricity involved.

Sushruta had students practise on vegetables first

The Sushruta Samhita says what to rehearse on before touching a patient. Incisions on gourds and melons. Punctures on a leather bag filled with water. Scraping on hides. What this workshop teaches is not new to India. It is about as old as surgery.

The most quoted line in the field is a warning about equipment

David Gaba's line is that simulation is a technique, not a technology. Quoted constantly. Ignored almost as often, usually by places that buy a mannequin and then wonder why nothing changed.

The evidence points at the conversation, not the kit

Across twenty years of reviews, one feature keeps coming out on top. Feedback and debriefing. Choose between a better mannequin and a better debrief, and buy the debrief. This session costs under a thousand rupees. Same point, made with money.

Why the session is built the way it is. Worth reading if you are thinking of running something like this yourself.

Why you will see the same case twice

Because the second time is where the room actually learns.

The first encounter is raw. You are watching a colleague do something difficult, partly watching the person rather than the behaviour, and nobody has handed you the vocabulary yet. You see it, but you do not see it clearly.

Then the room spends sixteen minutes taking it apart. By the time the second doctor walks in, you have been told what to look for and have practised naming it at your table. You watch with different eyes. That change in how you watch is the thing being taught.

Three other things it buys

  • The second doctor watched the first. So the question becomes: you saw what happened, what did you do differently, and what happened when you did?
  • One variable changes everything. Same case, same actor, same room. She withholds consent for one part. You see with your own eyes that a scenario is a machine with adjustable parts. That is what makes it something you could build.
  • You see the discussion run twice, by two people, with the same structure. So you can tell it is a technique, not a personality. Watch it once and you would think the facilitator is just good at it.

Why only two people take the chair

At two hours, rotating thirty people through would give everyone a few minutes and nobody a discussion worth having.

So the trade is made openly. Two people act. Twenty-eight watch with a specific job. What you take home is not the experience of being in the chair. It is having watched a discussion run properly, twice, closely enough to run one yourself.

Why your table has a job

Thirty people watching with nothing to do is an audience. Thirty people each watching for one specific thing is a faculty.

Two of the jobs will feel odd. One table watches only the survivor, not the doctor. Another watches as defence counsel, looking for what they would attack in court. Those two change how the whole room sees the case, and they usually produce the sharpest observation of the day.

Why nothing is scored

Teaching and examining are different jobs. Mixing them damages both.

The moment people think they are being marked, they perform instead of learning. Nothing today is scored. Nothing is reported to anyone. Nobody has to volunteer.

Why the examination is described, not done

Nothing intimate is examined on anyone. The doctor says each step out loud and points it out on a model.

Nothing that matters is lost by doing it this way. The sequence, the consent, the explanation before each step, the practices that must not happen: all of it can be tested out loud. What touching a person would add is realism nobody here needs.
