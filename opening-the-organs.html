Opening the organs

Step-by-step dissection of individual organs at autopsy: how each one comes out, how to hold it, where the cut goes, and why that plane and no other.

Fifteen organs, each laid out the same way. You get the block it leaves the body in and why, what to do before the first cut, how to hold the specimen, the accepted ways of cutting it and how to choose between them, the cautions, what to look for, what gets missed, the normal weights and measurements, and what to preserve.

The tool works at two levels. MBBS covers nine organs, with one accepted technique for each, the normal weights, and the mistakes people really make. MD and postgraduate covers all fifteen, with the alternative techniques and the reasons for choosing between them, the measurements, and six special dissections: the cardiac conduction system, the spinal cord, the vitreous, the middle and inner ear, the pituitary, and the pelvic organs. Each organ also has a checklist to use at the table and practice questions with worked answers.

Organ dissection at autopsy, organ by organ

Each organ below is set out in the same order used inside the tool: how it leaves the body, what to do before the first cut, how to hold it, the method of section, what to look for, what gets missed, normal weights and measurements, and what to preserve.

Heart (Cor) · MBBS

Pick the cut before you pick up the knife. Whatever it goes through, you won’t see intact again.

How the heart comes out

Most hearts, normal or with acquired disease, can come out on their own. It’s different when the disease runs beyond the heart, as with carcinoma of the lung or gullet (oesophagus), or a dissection of the ascending aorta. Then the chest organs come out together as one block (en bloc). In congenital heart disease, take the chest contents en bloc at any age. Free the heart and you cut the very vessels the disease was spreading along. After that, nobody can show where it went.

Before you cut

  • You may want a chest X-ray before the autopsy starts.
  • If there’s fluid in the pericardium (the sac around the heart), send it for culture. If there’s clot, weigh it. A weight turns cardiac tamponade, the heart squeezed by blood in its sac, into a number rather than an impression.
  • Not sure whether it’s blood or blood-stained fluid? A haematocrit on the sample settles it.
  • Before you dissect anything, check the coronary arteries for calcification and tortuosity (twisting). If angiography is wanted, it has to happen before the coronaries are cut, and ideally before fixation. The first cut into the vessel ends both chances.

How to hold it

For short-axis slicing, lay the heart on its flat underside (the diaphragmatic surface) on a paper towel. The towel grips, so the heart won’t slide under the knife.

Heart dissection: Inflow–outflow (the classical opening)

When: Best suited to normal hearts. It’s also how you open the basal third left over after short-axis slicing. You work one side at a time and follow the blood. Open the atrium first, then take the ventricle in along its inflow tract and out along its outflow tract. Where a cut crosses a valve, it passes between the commissures (the junctions where neighbouring leaflets meet), so those junctions stay intact.

  1. Right atrium. Run scissors from the inferior vena cava to the right atrial appendage. Keep clear of the superior vena cava and the sinus node area beside it.
  2. Right ventricle, inflow. From the right atrium, cut through the posterior tricuspid leaflet and carry on parallel to the back of the ventricular septum, about 1 cm from it. A knife or scissors will do.
  3. Right ventricle, outflow. Same approach, this time about 1 cm from the front of the septum. The cut goes through the anterior pulmonary cusp and on into the main pulmonary artery.
  4. Left atrium. Use scissors between the right and left upper pulmonary veins, then between the upper and lower veins on each side. Carry the cut into the left atrial appendage if you want to check it for mural thrombus (clot stuck to the wall).
  5. Left ventricle, inflow. Take a long knife along the inferolateral wall. It passes through the left atrial wall near the appendage, the middle of the posterior mitral leaflet, between the two papillary muscles, and out through the apex.
  6. Left ventricle, outflow. This cut runs parallel to the front of the septum, about 1 cm from it. It curves, so a scalpel handles it best. Then switch to scissors, cross the left aortic cusp and continue into the ascending aorta, passing to one side of the left coronary ostium (the opening of the left coronary artery).

Cautions:

  • On the outflow cut, stay out of the anterior mitral leaflet and the ventricular septum. A cut into either can’t be undone.
  • Once the chambers are open, stop. Slicing on into the muscle only damages what you still need to see.

Heart dissection: Short axis (the bread-slice method)

When: The method of choice in ischaemic heart disease, and it works for almost any other cardiac condition as well. Slices cut parallel to the atrioventricular groove show more heart muscle than any other plane. They also match the plane of a two-dimensional echocardiogram, so your findings line up with the clinical scan.

  1. Lay the flat diaphragmatic surface on a paper towel.
  2. With a sharp knife, cut slices 1.0 to 1.5 cm thick, parallel to the atrioventricular groove.
  3. Make each slice in one firm stroke, or two strokes in the same direction.
  4. Look at every slice from the apex towards the base. That’s the view the echocardiographer uses.
  5. Stop short of the basal third of the ventricles and leave it attached to the atria. Open that part afterwards by the inflow–outflow method.

Cautions:

  • Don’t saw back and forth. Each change of direction leaves a hesitation mark on the cut face, and someone may later read that mark as disease.

Coronary arteries. In someone under 30 who died of a non-cardiac cause, the coronaries may be opened lengthwise. In everyone else, cut across them every 3 to 5 mm. If a vessel is so calcified that the scalpel won’t go through, strip it off the heart and decalcify it for at least 24 hours before cutting.

Grading the obstruction. Narrowing is graded on a four-point scale, one grade for each quarter of cross-sectional area lost. Grade 4 means 75 per cent or more and counts as severe. A grade 4 lesion at 90 per cent is critical, and grade 4 lesions should be confirmed under the microscope. Severe disease of the left main coronary artery counts the same as two-vessel disease. So left main disease plus grade 4 in the other three vessels is severe four-vessel disease.

Repairing a mistake. Slice gone wrong? Glue the pieces back with cyanoacrylate (superglue) and recut in a better plane.

What to look for

  • Size, and which chambers are enlarged. Enlargement may be hypertrophy (thicker walls), dilatation (bigger chambers), or both.
  • Shape: a normal heart is conical, a globe-shaped or irregular one isn’t. An irregular outline suggests a ventricular aneurysm, a bulge in a weakened wall.
  • Colour of the muscle under the epicardium. An old infarct looks grey. Chronic anaemia makes the muscle pale. A fresh infarct or a rupture looks mottled or bloody.
  • How the left ventricle feels. Firmness can mean hypertrophy, fibrosis, amyloid or calcification, but rigor alone also makes it firm. Softness points to a fresh infarct, myocarditis, dilated cardiomyopathy or decomposition.
  • Mural thrombus in every dilated chamber. Clot tends to settle in the atrial appendages, at the ventricular apices and inside aneurysms, so look there.
  • The foramen ovale. An open (patent) foramen ovale lets a clot cross from the venous side and reach the brain, so record whether it’s open. To test it, pass a graduated probe from the right atrium between the valve and the rim (limbus) of the fossa ovalis, or from the left atrium at the ostium secundum.

What gets missed

  • The photograph before opening. After heart surgery, or in a cardiac death, put the heart in formalin for at least five minutes to take the shine off the surface. Then set it the way it lay in the chest and photograph it.
  • Rigor mortis in the heart. The heart stops in asystole (without contraction) and is limp at first, then stiffens within an hour. Its walls and chambers then look as they would at end-systole, fully contracted. By about 24 hours the rigor has passed, the wall thins and the chambers widen. That isn’t dilated cardiomyopathy, though it has been mistaken for it.
  • Wall thickness taken at the wrong level. The wall is thickest at the base and thinnest at the apex, so a fair average comes from the level of the papillary muscles. Measure the septum and right ventricle at that level too, and leave trabeculations and papillary muscles out of the figure.
  • Relying on wall thickness in a dilated heart. It isn’t reliable there. Go by total heart weight instead.

Normal weights and measurements: heart

  • Weighed to: nearest 1 g. In an adult, at least to the nearest 5 g. Before weighing, remove the parietal pericardium, trim the great vessels to about 2 cm and clear postmortem clot from the chambers. Under one year of age, use a scale that reads to 0.1 g.
  • Hypertrophy: LV >1.5 · RV >0.5 cm. For an adult heart that isn’t dilated and is in rigor. In a dilated heart these figures fail, so use the weight.
  • LV internal dimension: ≤2.5 cm. Short-axis view, average-sized adult, heart in rigor.
  • Athlete’s heart: +25%. Seldom more than a quarter above the expected weight. Neither the naked eye nor the microscope can tell it apart from pathological hypertrophy.
  • Ischaemic disease alone: <550 g. Mild hypertrophy of all four chambers, when there’s no hypertension as well.
  • Chronic pressure or volume load: 600–900 g. Often 2.0 to 2.5 times the expected weight. Seen with systemic hypertension, aortic stenosis, dilated or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and congenital disease.
  • Weights beyond: >1000 g. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, long-standing aortic regurgitation, and acromegaly with hypertension.
  • Isolated RV hypertrophy: <500 g. Pulmonary hypertension on its own rarely pushes the whole heart past this.
  • Fixation: weight: 5–10%. The shift in heart weight that fixation causes.
  • Fixation: LV thickness: +10%
  • Fixation: valve circumference: −10–25%. Perfusion fixation does the opposite and usually enlarges them, especially on the right side.
  • Fixation: chamber volume: −50%
  • Share of body weight: 0.40–0.45%
  • Normal at 70 kg, men: 327 g. Range 248 to 431 g. Heart weight follows body weight, so compare with the body in front of you, not a single figure.
  • Normal at 70 kg, women: 280 g. Range 191 to 411 g.

What to preserve

  • Cut transmural blocks (through the full thickness of the wall), about 1.5 cm wide and 0.3 cm thick. Make them rectangles, not wedges. A rectangle holds more subendocardial muscle, the inner layer where ischaemic damage shows first.
  • Label each block with the site it came from.
  • Stain coronary arteries and valves with Verhoeff–van Gieson, which picks out elastic tissue. Routine stains are enough for the rest.
  • A heart that looks normal, in a non-cardiac death, may need no slides at all, or just one section of left ventricle that includes a papillary muscle.

Source: Ludwig ch 3, Cardiovascular System (W. D. Edwards); weights from Part III.

Lungs and airway (Pulmones) · MBBS

Look for the embolus before you slice, because slicing is what destroys it.

How the lungs and airway comes out

When you take the neck organs out as a block, don’t stop at the trachea, larynx and cervical oesophagus (gullet). Take the floor of the mouth, the tongue, the soft palate and the tonsils with them. Stop the block at the larynx and anything important above it is lost.

Before you cut

  • Before you lift the lungs out, make a small cut beside the sternum to let air into the chest (a pneumothorax). This helps the lungs come out without tearing.
  • Cover the cut ends of the ribs with a thick towel or a plastic sheet. Sharp rib ends tear the pleura, and they cut the hands of everyone working on the body too.
  • Free any adhesions (fibrous bands between lung and chest wall) as close to the parietal pleura as you can. Expect the worst of them at the back of the lower lobes.

How to hold it

For the lateral-incision method, leave a cuff of bronchus and artery on the lung and hold the hilum (the root where the vessels and airway enter) in your hand.

Lungs and airway dissection: From the hilum

When: The quicker of the two methods. Start at the hilum, open the pulmonary arteries and bronchi outwards, and only then slice the lung.

  1. Open the pulmonary arteries and bronchi, working from the hilum out towards the edge of the mediastinal surface.
  2. Then cut the lung into several sagittal (front-to-back) slices, parallel to the mediastinal surface.

Cautions:

  • The lung no longer holds together as one piece, so it’s hard to say later where a particular slice came from.
  • Vessels and bronchi that run in a more frontal plane can’t be opened without partly wrecking the slices.

Lungs and airway dissection: From incisions along the lateral surface

When: Use it when you want the lung kept in one piece, so you can still prove where a lesion sits. Working from inside the lung, open the arterial tree and then the bronchial tree, spreading outwards like the spokes of a wheel. The hilum stays intact.

  1. Leave a cuff of bronchus and pulmonary vessels on the lung and hold the hilum in your hand.
  2. Cut each lobe from apex to base along its longest lateral axis. In the right middle lobe, that axis runs almost horizontally.
  3. Carry the upper and lower lobe cuts towards the hilum, but stop short of it.
  4. Join them with a third cut at right angles to the first two. This cut opens part of the wall of a main pulmonary artery, which you can usually see shining through the pleura in the interlobar fissure near the hilum.
  5. Slip one blade of the scissors into that opening and open the pulmonary arteries outwards in every direction. Carry each cut right out to the lung tissue and the pleura, so the lung can be laid open flat.
  6. Do the same for the bronchial tree. You’ll cut through many pulmonary artery branches on the way.

Cautions:

  • Expect this to take more practice than opening from the hilum.
  • If the lung was cut off the main bronchi too close to the hilum, keeping the hilar structures intact becomes difficult.

Lungs and airway dissection: Slicing a fixed lung

When: After perfusion fixation, where formalin has been run into the lung before slicing. Cut frontal or sagittal slices, each about 1.5 cm thick.

  1. For frontal slices, lay the lung with the hilum facing up and make the first cut right next to the hilum.
  2. Use a long blade and draw it through in one smooth pull.

Cautions:

  • A short knife needs several strokes, and each one leaves a mark across the face of the slice.

Larynx. The routine is to open it along the back midline and pull the two halves apart to show the lining (mucosa). In an adult that may mean cracking cartilage that has turned to bone (ossified). If the cartilage hasn’t ossified, or only slightly, you can cut the larynx into serial cross-sections instead. That gives good histology of the mucosa and of the cricoarytenoid joint, which sits just below the vocal cords on either side of the back midline.

Trachea and main bronchi. The routine is to open them along the soft back wall (the posterior membranous wall). In aspiration and drowning, open them from the front while they’re still in the body (in situ). A tracheo-oesophageal fistula, an abnormal opening between windpipe and gullet, shows best through a front midline cut, or by removing the whole front half of the trachea.

Fresh or fixed. Pulmonary oedema (fluid in the lungs) and pulmonary embolism are best judged in the fresh lung. A sensible middle path is to perfusion-fix one lung and dissect the other fresh. The fresh one also gives you material for microbiology and smears.

What to look for

  • The pulmonary arteries at the hilum, before any slicing. A clot (embolus) in a main branch is exactly what a careless slice destroys.
  • The hyoid bone, after suspected strangulation. Find it and check it for fracture.
  • The larynx for haemorrhages, after suspected strangulation
  • The cricoarytenoid joints. You have them at every autopsy. Where systemic arthritis is suspected, they’re worth studying along with the sternoclavicular joints.

What gets missed

  • Opening the trachea from behind in a drowning or an aspiration. The routine back cut is the wrong one here. Open it from the front, in situ.
  • Slicing the lung before anyone has looked for an embolus.
  • Stopping the neck block at the larynx, and losing the tongue, palate and floor of the mouth.

Normal weights and measurements: lungs and airway

  • Right lung: 450 g. Range 360 to 570.
  • Left lung: 375 g. Range 325 to 480.
  • Share of body weight: ~1%
  • Fixed slice: 1.5 cm

What to preserve

  • Use a container with three compartments for the right lobes and two for the left, so each lobe stays identifiable.
  • Whichever method you used, you should be able to tell where every section came from.

Source: Ludwig ch 4, Tracheobronchial Tree and Lungs; removal of the neck organs from ch 1; weights from Part III.

Neck structures (Regio cervicalis) · MBBS

Take the brain out first, or you will make the haemorrhage you then report.

How the neck structures comes out

The neck organs and the floor of the mouth can come out with the chest organs or as a separate block. Either is fine. Rushing it isn’t.

Before you cut

  • In a medicolegal autopsy, especially where strangulation is suspected, long skin incisions in the neck are both justified and permitted.
  • In these cases, TAKE THE BRAIN OUT FIRST. Removing the brain drains blood out of the neck. Without that, the dissection itself can cause bleeding (an artefactual haemorrhage) in the very tissue you’re about to report on.

How to hold it

Slowly. The source singles out this part of the autopsy as the one place where pace matters.

Neck structures dissection: The careful removal

When: Every time you take the floor of the mouth and the soft palate. There’s no special trick here. The danger is haste.

  1. Work slowly and deliberately through the soft palate and the floor of the mouth.
  2. Always know where your assistant’s hand is.

Cautions:

  • An inexperienced or hurried prosector tears the skin of the neck, and sometimes the lips.
  • It’s easy to cut or stab your assistant’s hand at this stage.
  • Don’t attempt it without an experienced teacher (preceptor) standing beside you.

What the posterior approach shows. With the body face down (prone), cut in the midline over the spinous processes, remove the muscles and take off the laminae on both sides (bilateral laminectomy). This opens up the back of the neck: muscles, ligaments, vertebrae and vertebral arteries. It shows deep bruises (contusions) with blood in the tissue, torn ligaments, and fractures at the back of the vertebral bodies. It’s the recommended approach whenever neck injury is suspected, including flexion and extension injuries. Morticians dislike it as a routine, because embalming fluid leaks out of the cut in the back.

What to look for

  • The hyoid bone, for fracture
  • The larynx, for haemorrhage
  • The cricoarytenoid joints
  • Whether any neck haemorrhage could have been made by the dissection itself. That question is the reason the brain comes out first.

What gets missed

  • Opening the neck before the brain is out. If you then find haemorrhage, you can’t say whether the injury or your knife caused it.
  • Injuring your assistant. The source warns about it plainly, and so should a teaching tool.

Source: Ludwig ch 1, Principles (J. Ludwig), and ch 4; the source points onward to Vanezis and to Adams ch 2 for the layered neck dissection itself.

Brain (Encephalon) · MBBS

Four points on the skull, and each one has a specific way of going wrong.

How the brain comes out

The brain comes out on its own, through a coronal scalp incision (ear to ear over the crown) and a cut around the skull vault with an oscillating saw. If you also need the neck organs or the nasal cavities, the brain has to come out first anyway.

Before you cut

  • Rest the head on a wooden block or the metal headrest.
  • Comb a parting in the hair along an imaginary line running from one mastoid, over the top of the head, to the other.
  • Bone dust can carry infection. Saw inside a plastic bag, and switch to a handsaw if Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease is suspected.

How to hold it

Cradle the brain in one palm as its own weight lets it fall away from the skull base.

Brain dissection: Opening the scalp

When: Always. A single coronal cut through the full thickness of the scalp, from behind one ear to behind the other.

  1. Cut through the full thickness of the scalp from the outside with a sharp scalpel.
  2. Start on the right, just behind the earlobe. Go as low as you can without dropping below the earlobe, and finish at the same level on the other side.
  3. Undercut the edges a little so you can grip them. Then fold the front half forward and the back half backward. A dry towel over the edge gives you most of the grip you need.
  4. Fold the front flap down to 1 to 2 cm above the brow ridge (supraorbital ridge), and the back flap down to just above the occipital protuberance.

Cautions:

  • Leave enough tissue behind the ear for the mortician to stitch.
  • If you need a blade to free the connective tissue, keep the edge facing the skull, never the scalp.

Brain dissection: Sawing the vault

When: Always. The saw line runs through four points. This shape stops the skull cap sliding when the embalmer puts it back.

  1. Point A, frontal: about two fingerbreadths above the brow ridges (supraorbital ridges).
  2. Point B, temporal: at the top of the ear in its natural position. Judge this before you fold the scalp back.
  3. Point C: about 2 cm above B.
  4. Point D, occipital: about two fingerbreadths above the external occipital protuberance.
  5. First cut the temporalis muscle with a sharp knife and clear it out of the saw’s path.
  6. Stop just short of the inner table (the inner layer of skull bone). A chisel and a light tap with a mallet will finish it.

Cautions:

  • Point A too low, and the saw enters the roof of the orbit.
  • Point B too low, and the saw enters the petrous temporal bone.
  • Either mistake makes the skull cap hard to lift off.
  • Point D too low, and the saw line falls below where the tentorium attaches.
  • Rest the index finger of your saw hand alongside the blade to judge how deep it’s going. Move the blade from side to side rather than pressing in one spot. Turn the head away from the side you’re cutting, so the brain sinks away from the vault.

Brain dissection: Detaching the brain

When: Always. Work from front to back and let the brain’s own weight open the field for you.

  1. Lift the frontal lobes gently and peel the olfactory bulbs and tracts off the cribriform plates.
  2. Cut the optic nerves where they enter the optic foramina.
  3. Let the brain fall away from the floor of the anterior cranial fossa under its own weight, resting on your palm.
  4. Cut the pituitary stalk, then the internal carotid arteries where they enter the skull.
  5. Cut cranial nerves III, IV, V and VI as close to the skull base as you can, along with the subdural communicating veins.
  6. With curved scissors, cut the tentorium along the petrous ridge on each side.
  7. Cut cranial nerves VII to XII, naming each one as you go.
  8. Cut the vertebral arteries where they enter the skull.
  9. With curved scissors, cut across the cervical cord as low as you can. Keep the cut from getting too oblique.

Cautions:

  • While you’re cutting the tentorium, don’t let the brain drop backwards. That’s what tears the cerebral peduncles. Raising the head high from the start, with the neck well flexed, prevents it.
  • Don’t leave the pineal body behind in the skull.
  • If you want to see the brain with cerebrospinal fluid still in the subarachnoid space, keep the dura intact while sawing. Open it along the saw line afterwards.
  • With an extradural or subdural haemorrhage, or a tumour, leave the dural flaps attached to the brain and section them together.

Brain dissection: Cutting the fixed brain

When: The proper way. Only a fixed brain allows a thorough examination. Take off the brainstem and cerebellum first, then cut the hemispheres into coronal slices.

  1. Weigh the brain fresh, and again after fixation.
  2. Mark the central sulci first. Nick the thin membranes (leptomeninges) bridging over them with the tip of a scalpel, without touching the brain underneath. That gives you a landmark on every slice you cut later.
  3. Now separate the brainstem and cerebellum. Turn the brain upside down. Hold the cerebellum between your index finger, with its tip near the pineal gland, and your thumb on the underside.
  4. Hold the scalpel like a pen, and rest your cutting hand on the underside of the frontal lobes to steady the angle. Enter the midbrain in the midline a few millimetres above the tip of the mammillary body, aiming at the pineal gland. Push just through, draw the blade towards you for half the midbrain, then turn it and go forward along the same plane for the other half.
  5. Hold the brain by its convexity (the curved upper surface), with the orbital lobes and occipital poles level. Make the first cut through the hemispheres at the mammillary body, and check the two cut faces for symmetry.
  6. Cut slices about 1 cm thick. Hold each half down on its cut surface and move the knife from the underside towards the convexity.
  7. Cut the brainstem at right angles to its long axis, adjusting the plane as you go, because the axis curves slightly. The cerebellum can be cut horizontally or vertically.
  8. Lay the slices out with the right side of the brain on your left. That’s how a scan is displayed, so the two can be compared directly.

Cautions:

  • One heavy sweep presses the slice into the board, squashes structures and drags vessels into the softer tissue. Use several light passes instead.
  • Hold the handle lightly. A tight grip is what leaves knife marks.
  • Never cut the midbrain from the side, over the temporal lobe. You end up with a roof-shaped midbrain and no usable cross-section.
  • Cut the midbrain too far forward (rostrally) and the cut comes out uneven, because the cerebral peduncles widen quickly there. Take a trial section near the junction of pons and midbrain, then a parallel one where you can see what you’re doing.
  • Look at each new cut face before the next slice, so you can correct the plane.
  • Give yourself enough room. Slices shouldn’t overlap on the board.
  • Prop up the optic nerve. If it hangs limp, it gets sliced lengthwise.

Fresh or fixed. Only a brain cut after proper fixation lets you recognise lesions reliably and match them to the scan. Cut it fresh only when microbiology or chemistry must come first, or when a diagnosis is needed at once. Be aware that cutting fresh distorts the cut surface during the fixation that follows. A middle path is three or four coronal cuts through the hemispheres, leaving the basal ganglia and upper brainstem untouched.

An alternative first cut. Instead of cutting at the mammillary bodies, the first cut can go just in front of the temporal poles to open the front (anterior) horns of the ventricles. In hydrocephalus this may show a blockage at the foramen of Monro, and you can still change the plane afterwards to show it better.

The knife. A single-blade autopsy knife about 25 cm long and 2 cm wide is enough. You don’t need a very large one.

Fetal and infant brains. Keep these whole until they’re properly fixed, because they’re soft and bruise easily. Where the sutures are open and the bones soft, open the skull with blunt scissors rather than a saw. Leave a midline strip about 1 cm wide, carrying the sagittal sinus and falx, and an intact patch of temporal bone (squama) on each side to act as a hinge.

What to look for

  • Symmetry of the two cut faces at the mammillary body cut
  • The arteries at the base of the fixed brain. Open the arachnoid and follow the arteries a short way, looking for thrombosis, embolism or aneurysm. Skip this if it would disturb a lesion in that area.

What gets missed

  • Leaving the pineal body behind in the skull.
  • Stripping the arteries off the brain as a routine. It does no good, and it separates the vascular lesion from the brain damage it caused.
  • Letting the brain drop back before the tentorium is cut, then reporting the resulting tears in the peduncles as an injury.

Normal weights and measurements: brain

  • Share of body weight: 1.4%
  • Normal, men: 1279–1396 g. Average values across adult age groups, highest in the twenties and falling with age. Full recorded range 1018 to 1685 g.
  • Normal, women: 1121–1242 g. Same pattern. Recorded range 820 to 1565 g. These figures come from a 1949 compilation, so treat them as a rough guide.
  • Slice thickness: 1 cm
  • Frontal saw point: 2 fingerbreadths. Above the supraorbital ridge.
  • Occipital saw point: 2 fingerbreadths. Above the external occipital protuberance.

What to preserve

  • Record the weight both fresh and after fixation.

Source: Ludwig ch 6, Nervous System (C. Giannini and H. Okazaki); weights from Part III.

Liver and biliary tract (Hepar) · MBBS

The duct work is dissected before the liver comes out, not after.

How the liver and biliary tract comes out

Dissect the hepatoduodenal ligament (the band carrying the bile duct, hepatic artery and portal vein) before the liver comes out. If you’re interested in the portal vein and its tributaries, or the inferior vena cava, dissect from behind and take the organs out together, en bloc or en masse. Cut the liver free first, and the relations inside that ligament become guesswork.

Before you cut

  • Cut into the common bile duct and open it in both directions: up towards the liver hilum and down towards the ampulla of Vater.
  • The lowest part of the duct runs behind the duodenum. To see it, pull the duodenum forwards and slightly to the left. Otherwise you’ll cut into the duodenal wall trying to reach it.
  • The hepatic artery lies to the left of the common bile duct and is easy to free from the front of the ligament.

How to hold it

Flat on a board, sliced from one end in a single pulling stroke.

Liver and biliary tract dissection: Frontal slices

When: The routine. Slices about 2 cm thick, cut in the frontal plane (parallel to the front of the body).

  1. Slice the whole liver in the frontal plane, each slice about 2 cm thick.
  2. You can leave the hilar structures attached to one of the central slices.

Cautions:

  • An ordinary knife almost always leaves marks on a sliced liver, and a cirrhotic liver is worse. A very long blade lets you cut through the whole organ in one smooth pull.

Liver and biliary tract dissection: Horizontal sections

When: When you want a large area of liver tissue on one face, or the hilar structures left intact. The same cut turned through ninety degrees.

  1. Cut horizontally rather than frontally.

Gallbladder. Usually the gallbladder comes out of its bed intact and is opened in a fine-mesh strainer over a collecting dish, so bile doesn’t spill and stain the other organs. If liver and gallbladder are to be fixed together, first draw the bile out of the unopened gallbladder with a syringe. Then partly open the gallbladder and the ducts outside the liver, and pack them with formalin-soaked cotton so they hold their shape in the bath. The cystic duct is very hard to dissect because of its many folds.

Gallstones. Some stones can be cut fresh. Many need 24 hours in strong formalin to harden first. If they’re still too hard, a fine scroll saw gives a cut surface worth studying.

In the newborn. Dissecting the common bile duct in a newborn is difficult. It’s easier to test whether the duct is open (patent) by opening the duodenum and seeing whether bile can be squeezed out through the papilla. That’s a useful test when biliary atresia is suspected.

What to look for

  • The cut surface, for the nodules of cirrhosis
  • The confluence of splenic, portal and superior mesenteric veins. This junction shows best from behind.

What gets missed

  • Slicing the liver before the bile ducts have been opened.
  • A thick fresh slice left in formalin, where the fixative never reaches the middle. Thin slices of 3 to 4 mm fix easily but distort a lot.

Normal weights and measurements: liver and biliary tract

  • Liver, male: 1.9–3.0%. Of body weight.
  • Liver, female: 2.2–2.9%. Of body weight.
  • Slice thickness: 2 cm

Source: Ludwig ch 5, Esophagus and Abdominal Viscera; weights from Part III.

Kidneys and ureters (Renes) · MBBS

Strip the capsule while the vessels are still attached, and the kidney stops slipping out of your hand.

How the kidneys and ureters comes out

Routinely, the kidneys are cut into while still in the body (in situ). They can then be freed at the vessels and ureter, or taken out together with the aorta, inferior vena cava and pelvic organs. When a process behind the peritoneum involves more than one organ, as with a ruptured aortic aneurysm or a renal carcinoma growing into the veins, taking everything out as one block is the only way to dissect it properly.

Before you cut

  • Open the renal vessels lengthwise from the aorta or the inferior vena cava to the hilum.

How to hold it

Keep the renal vessels under tension. They’re your anchor and your grip.

Kidneys and ureters dissection: The routine incision

When: Always. Strip the capsule, then make one cut from the outer border in to the pelvis.

  1. Strip off the fibrous capsule and the surrounding fat, using the uncut renal vessels as an anchor.
  2. Cut each kidney from its convex outer border towards the hilum, opening the renal pelvis.
  3. Keep a firm hold by pulling gently on the renal vessels as you cut.
  4. Open the ureters lengthwise, starting at the renal pelvis. If you have to, cut through the kidney tissue at the lower pole to follow them.

Cautions:

  • Strip the capsule after the vessels are cut, and the kidney tends to slip out of your hand and onto the floor.

What to look for

  • The cortex, medulla and pelvis, all on the same cut face
  • The renal veins and the inferior vena cava. A renal cell carcinoma can grow along them into the cava.

What gets missed

  • The ureters. They should be opened from the pelvis downwards, yet they’re the part most often left unopened.

Normal weights and measurements: kidneys and ureters

  • Kidneys, male: 313 g. Combined. Range 230 to 440.
  • Kidneys, female: 288 g. Combined. Range 240 to 350.
  • Share of body weight: 0.3%
  • Size: 11.5×5.5×3.5 cm

What to preserve

  • Each block should include cortex, medulla with a papilla, and a piece of the renal pelvis.

Source: Ludwig ch 5, Esophagus and Abdominal Viscera; weights from Part III.

Spleen (Lien) · MBBS

Fixative does not get into the pulp, so the slice has to be thin.

How the spleen comes out

The spleen comes out with the abdominal organs and is sliced on the board, just like the liver.

How to hold it

The same as the liver: flat on the board.

Spleen dissection: Frontal or horizontal sections

When: The routine. The same principles as for the liver.

  1. Cut frontal or horizontal sections with a long blade.

Cautions:

  • If the slices are going into fixative, cut them very thin. Formalin doesn’t penetrate splenic pulp well.
  • Running formalin through the splenic vessels of an intact spleen won’t work unless the blood has been washed out first. Otherwise patches stay unfixed.

Washing out the pulp. First perfuse 0.9 per cent saline through the splenic artery or vein. At an injection pressure of about 100 mm Hg, the pulp turns white in about an hour. Then switch to 10 per cent formalin. This is also the best way to study the splenic reticulum, the spleen’s supporting framework.

What to look for

  • The cut surface of the pulp

What gets missed

  • A thick slice dropped into formalin. It comes back unfixed in the middle.

  • Share of body weight: 0.16%
  • Normal, men: ~145 g. Falls with age: about 190 g at 15 to 24 years, about 145 g from 40 onward. Recorded range across the bands 70 to 280.
  • Normal, women: ~115 g. About 120 g up to 59 years, about 110 g beyond. Recorded range 55 to 195.

Gullet, stomach and intestine (Oesophagus, gaster, intestinum) · MBBS

Take the histology before the specimen goes under the running tap.

How the gullet, stomach and intestine comes out

If a tumour or other lesion involves the duodenum, the papilla of Vater, the head of the pancreas or the hepatoduodenal ligament, open the duodenum while it’s still in the body. Once those organs are out, you may not be able to orient them precisely. This matters most after surgery.

Before you cut

  • Cut the mesentery close to the wall of the small intestine. The enterotome then runs smoothly along the bowel.

How to hold it

In a sink under running water, but only after the histology samples are taken.

Gullet, stomach and intestine dissection: Opening the gullet

When: Routine, with special steps for a fistula or varices. The gullet is opened along its back wall. A fistula and varices each need their own approach.

  1. For a tracheo-oesophageal fistula, open the gullet along its back wall and the trachea from the front.
  2. For varices (dilated veins), leave the gullet attached to the stomach and open the stomach along the greater curvature. Tie a string to the upper end of the unopened gullet and pull it down through the lumen, turning the gullet inside out. The varices then show through the mucosa, and formalin fixation makes them stand out even more.

Cautions:

  • If there’s an infiltrating tumour or a fistula, leave the gullet attached to the mediastinal organs.

Gullet, stomach and intestine dissection: Opening the stomach

When: Routine. Open it along the greater curvature.

  1. Open along the greater curvature.
  2. Don’t open it if there’s a penetrating ulcer or an infiltrating tumour. Instead, fix and section the stomach together with the pancreas, a piece of liver, or whatever the disease has spread into.

Cautions:

  • Even so, the gastric mucosa often looks poor under the microscope because of autolysis, the tissue’s self-digestion after death.

Gullet, stomach and intestine dissection: Opening the intestine

When: Routine. Use an enterotome: large scissors with one blunt blade that runs inside the bowel.

  1. Run the enterotome along the bowel, blunt blade inside.
  2. Work in a sink under running water.

Cautions:

  • Take histology samples BEFORE the bowel touches tap water.

Orientation for histology. Cut long strips of stomach or bowel lining parallel to the organ’s long axis, fix them, and embed them rolled into a spiral with the upper (proximal) end at the centre. Pin any separate piece of gut flat on cork or card before fixing, so it can be embedded and cut on edge.

The stationary enterotome. The source no longer uses or recommends it, because people get hurt cleaning it.

What to look for

  • Stomach contents. Note the volume, the smell and how far digestion has got. Normally the stomach empties a medium-sized meal in about three hours, and a heavy meal takes longer. The trap: significant head injury can delay emptying for days. Starchy food such as potato and bread is broken down quickly by swallowed salivary amylase. Vegetables and meat stay recognisable for a few hours, and mushrooms outlast everything.
  • The pattern of the lining (mucosa)

What gets missed

  • Taking histology after the tap. The lining is already fragile at autopsy, and running water finishes it off.
  • Opening the duodenum after removal in a post-operative case, and losing the orientation.

Normal weights and measurements: gullet, stomach and intestine

  • Oesophagus: 25 cm
  • Duodenum: 30 cm
  • Small intestine: 550–650 cm
  • Colon: 150–170 cm

What to preserve

  • Always pin gut flat on cork or card.

Source: Ludwig ch 5, Esophagus and Abdominal Viscera; gastric emptying from ch 2 (V. I. Adams); lengths from Part III.

Cardiac conduction system (Systema conducens cordis) · MD / PG

Two rectangles of tissue, and the sinus node is not one you can see.

How the cardiac conduction system comes out

Blocks are cut from the opened right side of the heart, once the ventricles have been dealt with. This examination is done entirely under the microscope. Nothing here can be judged by eye.

Before you cut

  • First ask whether it’s needed at all. In practice it rarely is. The usual reasons are a non-traumatic death with negative toxicology and no anatomical cause, or complete heart block.

How to hold it

If you cut right-handed, turn the heart 180 degrees so the right atrium is nearer you than the right ventricle, and press the left-sided chambers against the board.

Cardiac conduction system dissection: Sinus node block

When: Whenever the conduction system is being examined. Four cuts mark out a rectangle. Serial sections from it then cross the sinus node artery.

  1. First cut: with scissors, just in front of the terminal crest (crista terminalis), through the pectinate muscles, up to the upper border of the right atrial appendage.
  2. Second cut: at right angles to the first, along that upper border and into the superior vena cava.
  3. Third cut: roughly at right angles to the second and parallel to the first, along the line where the right atrial wall meets the atrial septum. It runs from the superior vena cava towards the inferior and is about 2 cm long.
  4. Fourth cut: this closes the rectangle.
  5. With a scalpel, cut 6 to 8 sections from the block, parallel to the second and fourth cuts. These cross the sinus node artery, which you can usually see with the naked eye.
  6. Two or three cassettes, numbered in order, usually hold them all.

Cautions:

  • You can’t identify the sinus node itself by eye or by any gross method.

Cardiac conduction system dissection: Atrioventricular block

When: The same indications. Take a rectangle spanning the triangle of Koch, the landmark area that holds the AV node, then cut it into serial 3 mm sections.

  1. Position the heart with the right-sided chambers open, right atrium above and right ventricle below.
  2. With a scalpel, remove a rectangular block about 2.0 cm high. It runs sideways from the opening of the coronary sinus to the far right edge of the membranous septum.
  3. Angle the block so the tricuspid valve ring (annulus) runs upward across it. That way about 1.5 cm of atrial septum is included at the coronary sinus end, and about 1.5 cm of ventricular septum at the membranous septum end.
  4. Trim the valve leaflets back to within 0.5 cm of their rings.
  5. Cut 6 to 10 sections about 3 mm thick. Start at the coronary sinus end and work towards the membranous septum.
  6. Put them into cassettes in that order, labelled AV-1, AV-2 and so on. Each cassette takes one to three pieces, depending on how thick the septum is.

Cautions:

  • The block will include much of the septal tricuspid leaflet and parts of the mitral and aortic valves. Only the pulmonary valve should be left uncut.

Lugol’s iodine in situ. Because the left bundle branch is rich in glycogen, Lugol’s iodine can show it in the opened heart, but only within 90 minutes of death. The AV bundle and the first part of the right bundle lie too deep for the stain, and the sinus node can’t be shown this way at all.

Gross dissection. You can see the AV node, the bundle of His and the right bundle branch by gross dissection, but it adds nothing useful to the diagnosis.

How many levels. Usually one level per paraffin block is enough. Cut several levels only for a specific question, such as injury from a medical procedure. Cutting through a whole block and keeping every tenth to fortieth section is research, not routine.

What to look for

  • Collagen in the sinus node. Between 10 and 90 years, the normal percentage of collagen in the sinus node roughly equals the person’s age. Use that as your yardstick.

What gets missed

  • Doing it when there’s no reason to.

Normal weights and measurements: cardiac conduction system

  • Sinus node sections: 6–8. Parallel to cuts 2 and 4, taking the sinus node artery in cross-section.
  • AV sections: 6–10. About 3 mm thick, labelled AV-1 onward from the coronary ostium side.
  • Sinus node collagen: = age. Between 10 and 90 years, the percentage of collagen normally expected is about the same as the age in years.

What to preserve

  • Stain with trichrome or Verhoeff–van Gieson. Collagen wraps and insulates the conduction tissue, and these stains show that collagen up.

Source: Ludwig ch 3, Cardiovascular System (W. D. Edwards).

Spinal cord (Medulla spinalis) · MD / PG

Ten to fifteen minutes with an oscillating saw, and the source says it belongs in every autopsy.

How the spinal cord comes out

There are two approaches: from behind, with the body turned face down, or from the front after evisceration, without turning the body at all. This isn’t a matter of taste. The deciding question is whether the neck is under suspicion.

Before you cut

  • Decide the approach before the body is moved.
  • For the front approach, finish removing the organs first.

How to hold it

Grip the freed spine in your left hand and pull it towards you. That makes the next cuts easy.

Spinal cord dissection: Posterior approach

When: Suspected neck injury, including flexion and extension injuries. Also craniocervical instability, excising an occipital encephalocele, showing an Arnold–Chiari malformation in place, or removing a meningomyelocele. With the body face down, remove the vertebral arches from behind (laminectomy).

  1. Turn the body face down with blocks under the shoulders. Flex the head forward and put towels under the face so it isn’t marked.
  2. Make a midline incision over the spinous processes.
  3. Remove the muscles.
  4. Saw through the laminae on both sides.

Cautions:

  • Morticians object to this approach as a routine, because embalming fluid leaks out of the cut in the back. If the body is to be embalmed, use it only when it’s really needed.

Spinal cord dissection: Anterior approach

When: The routine. It’s simple and quick, the body doesn’t need turning, and the cord and peripheral nerves can come out in one piece. Saw down both sides of the vertebral bodies, changing the blade angle as the shape of the vertebrae changes.

  1. Make the first cut across the top of the thoracic spine, at T1 or T2.
  2. Let the head drop back by removing the head support, or put a wooden block under the middle of the back. Either one straightens the spine.
  3. Next, cut down both sides of the upper thoracic spine, below the first cut, for about 10 to 15 cm.
  4. Saw a short stretch on one side, then the other, rather than finishing one side first.
  5. In the lumbar region, change the blade angle to follow the shape of the vertebrae.
  6. Before sawing, cut the muscles away from the spine down to where the nerves emerge, without cutting the nerves.
  7. Lift out the cord and cauda equina, still inside the dura, with as many spinal ganglia as you can.
  8. Tie a string and label around the L4 or L5 nerve root so you can identify the segments later.

Cautions:

  • STOP SAWING THE MOMENT YOU FEEL THE BONE GIVE. Otherwise the blade goes into the cord.
  • If you finish one side first, you can’t tell whether your cut line is in the right place. Alternating sides lets you catch a bad cut early.
  • If the line drifts towards the midline as you go up, it ends in a point and damages the cervical cord.
  • Tilting the blade too far sideways cuts the cord.

Getting the upper cervical cord out. Reach it from inside the skull. Cut the dura in a circle around the foramen magnum, then peel it off the bone downwards, holding it taut with a haemostat or forceps. Long scissors are usually all you need.

Routine sectioning. Open the dura along the front midline, look at the surface of the cord, then cut a series of cross-sections. Mark the right side of the cord with India ink before you start.

What to look for

  • Deep bruising with blood in the tissues, seen on the back approach
  • Torn or damaged ligaments
  • Fractures at the back of the vertebral bodies
  • The vertebral arteries
  • The spinal canal itself, once the cord is out

What gets missed

  • The whole cord. The source notes that general pathologists have long neglected it, and says it should be part of every autopsy.

Normal weights and measurements: spinal cord

  • Time needed: 10–15 min. With an oscillating saw. The source says the cord should be part of every autopsy.
  • Spinal cord: 27 g · 45 cm
  • First anterior cut: T1–T2
  • Each side cut: 10–15 cm. Alternate sides rather than taking one side all the way down.

What to preserve

  • Label the L4 or L5 root before the cord leaves the body.

Source: Ludwig ch 6, Nervous System (C. Giannini and H. Okazaki); weight from Part III.

Eye and vitreous (Oculus) · MD / PG

The needle goes in obliquely, and if it touches the retina the potassium reading is worthless.

How the eye and vitreous comes out

Vitreous humour, the clear gel inside the eyeball, is sampled with the eye in place. The globe itself is removed from the front unless the orbit is diseased too. Sampling works best on an intact eye with no known internal disease, such as a retinal detachment.

Before you cut

  • Before sampling, make sure there’s no known disease inside the eye.

How to hold it

For removal, hold the eyelids apart with retractors.

Eye and vitreous dissection: Vitreous sampling

When: Toxicology, biochemistry, microbiology, and an estimate of the interval since death. A single slanting needle pass through the pars plana, the flat band of the eye wall behind the iris that is safe to cross.

  1. Insert a 15-gauge needle at a slant through the sclera, 5 mm out from the limbus (the edge of the cornea).
  2. The needle passes through the pars plana into the vitreous.
  3. Draw out the sample.

Cautions:

  • DAMAGED RETINAL CELLS GIVE A FALSELY HIGH POTASSIUM. Vitreous potassium is what the rough estimate of time since death rests on. So a careless needle doesn’t just spoil the sample, it gives a wrong answer that still looks right.

Eye and vitreous dissection: Removing the globe, anterior approach

When: The usual way. Cut the attachments at the limbus, leaving enough muscle stump to orient the globe later.

  1. Hold the eyelids apart with retractors.
  2. With curved scissors, cut the conjunctiva free at the limbus.
  3. Leave Tenon’s capsule, the sheath around the eyeball, intact.
  4. Cut the four rectus muscles, leaving about 5.0 mm of each attached to the globe.
  5. Cut the inferior oblique.
  6. Pull on the inferior oblique stump to turn the eye outwards. This brings the optic nerve into reach, so you can take a long piece of it from inside the orbit.
  7. Dry the socket with a towel and fit a silastic mould.

Cautions:

  • Take care not to cut the eyelids while freeing the conjunctiva.
  • Keeping Tenon’s capsule intact is what stops fluid leaking into the empty socket.
  • From the front you can’t examine the rest of the orbit or the lacrimal gland. If the orbit is diseased, go in through the skull instead.

What to look for

  • Whether the vitreous sample is clear. The source doesn’t describe what a damaged sample looks like. It does state the consequence: retinal damage gives a falsely high potassium.

What gets missed

  • The 5 mm. Enter at the limbus instead of 5 mm out from it, and the needle ends up in the wrong compartment.

Normal weights and measurements: eye and vitreous

  • Needle: 15-gauge
  • Entry point: 5 mm. Lateral to the limbus, crossing the pars plana.
  • Angle: oblique
  • Muscle stump left: 5.0 mm. On each rectus, so the globe can be oriented later.

What to preserve

  • A clean sample gives a potassium you can use for a rough time since death. A contaminated one can’t be used.

Source: Ludwig ch 7, The Eye and Adnexa (R. J. Campbell).

Middle and inner ear (Auris media et interna) · MD / PG

Look into it whenever there is inflammation inside the skull, even when you are not taking it out.

How the middle and inner ear comes out

Once the brain is out, the ear is removed from inside the skull as a block of petrous bone. You don’t have to remove it to examine it, though. The source of an infection may be sitting in the ear, and a rongeur (bone-nibbling forceps) reaches it in seconds.

Before you cut

  • The brain has to be out first.
  • Even when there’s no reason to remove the hearing and balance organs whole, still look into the middle and inner ear. The source calls this good practice whenever there’s inflammation inside the skull. A large rongeur applied to the back outer part of the petrous ridge is all it takes.

How to hold it

Lift the freed block with bone-holding forceps, not your fingers.

Middle and inner ear dissection: The quick look (no removal)

When: Good practice whenever the ear isn’t being removed, especially if there’s inflammation inside the skull. Open the petrous ridge and look. Nothing comes out.

  1. Use a large rongeur on the posterolateral part (back and outer side) of the petrous ridge.
  2. Inspect the middle and inner ear.

Cautions:

  • This is the step that finds the ear as the source of a meningitis or brain abscess. Skip it, and that source never gets recorded.

Middle and inner ear dissection: Total removal (three saw lines)

When: When the ears need to be removed. Three saw cuts in the petrous bone mark out a block, taken with an oscillating saw.

  1. Line 1: as close to the tip (apex) of the petrous bone as you can, roughly at right angles to its upper edge.
  2. Line 2: over the mastoid region, as close to the side wall of the skull as you can.
  3. Line 3: with the blade held vertical to the floor.
  4. Lift the cut block out with bone-holding forceps.
  5. With curved scissors, cut the connective tissue bands holding it in place.
  6. Tie off the stump of the internal carotid artery, or simply plug it with clay.

Cautions:

  • Once the temporal bone is free, go carefully with chisel and hammer.
  • Plugging or tying the carotid stump takes a moment, and it saves the embalmer a mess.

Middle and inner ear dissection: Bone-plug cutter

When: An alternative to the three saw lines. A bone-plug cutter fitted to the vibrating saw takes out a round block.

  1. Fit the bone-plug cutter to the vibrating saw and cut the block out as a circle.

Fixation, on the Temporal Bone Bank schedule. Put the specimen in about 400 mL of 20 per cent formalin in a refrigerator for one day. Then change to fresh 10 per cent formalin daily for two more days. Kept refrigerated, the specimen lasts indefinitely. After a short decalcification it can be sliced and processed for light microscopy.

While you are in there. The same route through the skull opens the paranasal sinuses. Break the cribriform plate with chisel and mallet to reach the ethmoid sinuses, and keep chiselling to get into the maxillary sinuses. Reach the frontal sinuses by chiselling away their back walls near the midline. The sphenoidal sinuses come into view once the front wall and floor of the pituitary fossa are exposed, and even better if the pituitary block is sawn out.

What to look for

  • The middle and inner ear, whenever there’s inflammation inside the skull. The source of the infection may be there.
  • The carotid stump, before you walk away. Make sure it’s tied or plugged.

What gets missed

  • The quick look. It costs nothing, and it can turn an unexplained meningitis into an explained one.
  • Leaving the carotid stump open for the embalmer to deal with.

Normal weights and measurements: middle and inner ear

  • Fixation, day 1: 400 mL. 20 per cent formalin, refrigerated.
  • Fixation, days 2–3: 10%. Fresh formalin, daily.
  • Storage: indefinite. Refrigerated.

What to preserve

  • Short decalcification, then slice and process for light microscopy.

Source: Ludwig ch 6, Nervous System (C. Giannini and H. Okazaki).

Pituitary gland (Hypophysis cerebri) · MD / PG

Either take the fossa with it, or go in for the gland alone. The first also opens the sinuses.

How the pituitary gland comes out

There are two ways. Either saw out a five-sided (pentagonal) block of bone with the gland still inside its fossa, or free the gland alone by sharp dissection. The bone block also exposes the sphenoidal sinuses better than any other approach, so it does two jobs at once.

Before you cut

  • Remove the brain first, as usual.

How to hold it

Keep the saw blade roughly at right angles to the bone surface throughout.

Pituitary gland dissection: With its bony encasement (the pentagonal block)

When: The fuller approach, and the one that opens the sphenoidal sinuses. Five saw lines around the fossa, with the blade at right angles to the bone at every one.

  1. With an oscillating saw, cut out a five-sided block of bone around the pituitary fossa.
  2. At each of the five lines, keep the blade roughly at right angles to the bone.
  3. Lift out the block with the gland inside.

Cautions:

  • The blade angle is the whole technique. Come in at a slant on any line and the block won’t lift out cleanly.

Pituitary gland dissection: The gland alone

When: When the bony block is not wanted. Reach the fossa from above after removing the frontal lobes.

  1. Remove the frontal lobes with a coronal cut at the level of the lamina terminalis.
  2. Free the pituitary from its fossa by sharp dissection.
  3. If you need to, nibble away some bone with a small rongeur.
  4. Then remove the rest of the brain as usual.

Cautions:

  • This method sacrifices the frontal lobes as an intact specimen. Decide before you cut, not after.

What else it opens. Once the front wall and floor of the pituitary fossa are exposed, you can inspect the sphenoidal sinuses. Sawing out the bony block exposes them even better. Carry the dissection further and you reach the nasopharynx and throat.

What to look for

  • The gland within its fossa
  • The sphenoidal sinuses, now that they are open

What gets missed

  • The sinuses. This approach opens them for you, and they’re easy to overlook.

Normal weights and measurements: pituitary gland

  • Pituitary, 10–20 yr: 0.56 g
  • Pituitary, 20–70 yr: 0.61 g
  • In pregnancy: 0.95 g
  • Size: 2.1×1.4×0.5 cm
  • Pineal, for comparison: 0.14 g. Range 0.10 to 0.18. Size 0.7 × 0.45 × 0.4 cm.

Source: Ludwig ch 6, Nervous System (C. Giannini and H. Okazaki); weights from Part III.

Bladder and pelvic organs (Vesica urinaria et organa pelvis) · MD / PG

Fix it full, then take the top off.

How the bladder and pelvic organs comes out

The bladder can stay attached to the kidneys and ureters, or be handled as part of the pelvic block. For urography (contrast imaging of the urinary tract), the ureter has to be cannulated, either from inside the bladder or through the wall of the lower ureter.

Before you cut

  • Draw off the urine before anything else.

How to hold it

Keep it hanging and filled until fixation is complete.

Bladder and pelvic organs dissection: Distended fixation

When: Enlarged prostate (prostatic hyperplasia) causing obstruction, or tumours at the trigone. Fix the bladder in the shape it had in life, then open it.

  1. Drain the urine.
  2. Run formalin in through a catheter to fill the bladder.
  3. Leave it intact until fixation is complete.
  4. Then cut off the upper half to expose the base.

Cautions:

  • Open it fresh and flat, and you lose the relations at the trigone, which is exactly where the lesion is.

Bladder and pelvic organs dissection: Frontal sections through the base

When: Certain tumours and abscesses. Cut through the base of the bladder and the prostate together, in the frontal plane.

  1. Cut frontal sections through the base of the bladder and the prostate.

What to look for

  • The trigone
  • The prostate, in continuity with the bladder base

What gets missed

  • Emptying and flattening the bladder before anyone has looked at its base.

Normal weights and measurements: bladder and pelvic organs

  • Uterus, nulliparous: 35 g. Range 33 to 41. Size 7.9 × 3.9 × 2.3 cm.
  • Uterus, multiparous: 110 g. Range 102 to 117. Size 9.0 × 5.7 × 3.4 cm.
  • Ovary: 7 g. Size 3.4 × 1.5 × 0.8 cm.
  • Testis: 25 g. Range 20 to 27. Size 4.5 × 3.0 × 2.4 cm.
  • Prostate: 18 → 40 g. 18 g at 21 to 25 years, 20 g at 51 to 60, 40 g at 71 to 80. Size 3.6 × 2.8 × 1.9 cm.
  • Seminal vesicle: 4.3×1.7×0.9 cm

Source: Ludwig ch 5, Esophagus and Abdominal Viscera; weights from Part III.

The four classical autopsy techniques: Virchow, Rokitansky, Ghon and Letulle

  • Virchow (organ by organ). Organs come out one at a time. It’s the most widely used method, usually with some changes. Originally the skull was opened first, then the spinal cord from behind, then the chest, neck and abdominal organs in that order. Whenever no disease process needs its blood supply kept intact, which is most cases.
  • Rokitansky (in situ dissection). The organs are dissected inside the body, sometimes combined with removing blocks of organs. Only second-hand descriptions of the method survive. The name is often misused. Many pathologists say “Rokitansky’s technique” when they mean the removal methods of Ghon or Letulle, and that’s wrong.
  • Ghon (en bloc). The organs come out in three blocks: chest and neck organs, abdominal organs, and the urogenital system. Modified versions are widely used today. Some departments use it routinely. Others use it when a disease process makes it worth keeping the blood supply intact.
  • Letulle (en masse). Chest, neck, abdominal and pelvic organs all come out as one mass, which is then divided into organ blocks. The dissection is worked from the back of the organ mass. Best when disease involves or crosses the diaphragm; the source gives acute aortic dissection as the example. It’s also recommended for rare malformations in fetuses and infants, such as anomalous pulmonary venous connections. It takes more experience than the others, and the organ mass is awkward to handle. Even so, the body can go to the undertaker within 30 minutes without rushing the dissection.

Practice questions on autopsy dissection technique

Heart: which cut

A 62-year-old man collapses at work, and the clinical question is myocardial infarction. The heart is out and the coronaries have been cut across every 4 mm. How do you open the ventricles?

Answer: Short axis, parallel to the atrioventricular groove. Short-axis slicing is the method of choice in ischaemic heart disease. The slices show the most muscle, and they match the plane of an echocardiogram. Leave the basal third attached to the atria and open that part by the inflow–outflow method afterwards. (Ludwig ch 3)

Neck structures: what was missed

A 30-year-old woman is found dead in a bathroom with a ligature mark on her neck. The prosector opens the neck first, finds bleeding in the strap muscles, and records it as a finding. What went wrong?

Answer: The brain should have been removed first. In suspected strangulation, the brain comes out first so blood drains from the neck. Otherwise nobody can tell bleeding caused by the dissection from bleeding that happened in life, and the finding is worthless in court. (Ludwig ch 1)

Lungs and airway: which cut

A young man is pulled out of a canal, and drowning is the question. The neck block is in front of you. How do you open the trachea?

Answer: Anteriorly, in situ. Opening along the posterior membranous wall is the routine. In aspiration and drowning, though, the trachea should be opened from the front, in situ. (Ludwig ch 4)

Brain: what was missed

After removing a brain, the prosector reports tears in the cerebral peduncles. Nothing at the scene or in the history explains them. Where did they come from?

Answer: The brain was allowed to drop backwards while the tentorium was being cut. If the brain drops back too far while the tentorium is being cut, the peduncles stretch and tear. Raising the head high from the start, with the neck well flexed, prevents it. (Ludwig ch 6)

Gullet, stomach and intestine: what was missed

A bowel specimen is opened under running water, and the blocks are taken afterwards. The report says the mucosa can’t be assessed. Why?

Answer: The blocks were taken after the specimen met tap water. Histology samples should be taken before the bowel meets tap water. The lining is already fragile at autopsy, and running water finishes what autolysis started. (Ludwig ch 5)

Kidneys and ureters: which cut

You’re taking out the kidneys. When should you strip the capsule?

Answer: While the renal vessels are still attached. The uncut renal vessels act as an anchor. Strip the capsule while they’re attached, and the kidney won’t slip out of your hand once it’s free of the fat. (Ludwig ch 5)

Heart: what was missed

A heart is examined 30 hours after death. The left ventricular wall is thin and all four chambers are dilated, and the prosector reports dilated cardiomyopathy. Heart weight is normal for body size. Is that conclusion safe?

Answer: No, rigor mortis remits at about 24 hours and the chambers dilate then. The heart stops without contracting (asystole), stiffens within an hour, and its walls then look as they would at end-systole. At about 24 hours rigor passes, the wall thins again and the chambers dilate. The source warns specifically against mistaking this for dilated cardiomyopathy. A normal heart weight also argues against it, since weight is the reliable guide once a heart is dilated. (Ludwig ch 3)

Eye and vitreous: what was missed

Vitreous is drawn to measure potassium, and the sample is faintly bloodstained. The lab reports a high potassium, and time since death is estimated from it. What’s wrong with that estimate?

Answer: Damage to retinal cells raises the potassium falsely. Damaged retinal cells give a falsely high potassium. Because potassium carries the whole estimate, a careless needle doesn’t just lose the answer. It gives a wrong one that still looks convincing. Go in at a slant, 5 mm out from the limbus, through the pars plana. (Ludwig ch 7)

Cardiac conduction system: which cut

A 25-year-old dies suddenly. Toxicology is negative and there’s no anatomical cause of death, so you decide to examine the conduction system. Where does the atrioventricular block come from?

Answer: The triangle of Koch, between the coronary sinus ostium and the membranous septum. The AV node sits just above the tricuspid valve ring, between the coronary sinus opening and the membranous septum, inside the triangle of Koch. The block is about 2.0 cm high and spans those two landmarks. The terminal crest is the landmark for the sinus node block, not this one. (Ludwig ch 3)

Spinal cord: which cut

A body arrives after a fall from height, with a suspected flexion injury of the neck. Which approach do you use for the spinal cord?

Answer: Posterior, with the body prone. The back approach exposes the neck muscles, ligaments, vertebrae and vertebral arteries. It shows deep bruising with blood in the tissues, ligament injuries and fractures at the back of the vertebral bodies. That’s why it’s recommended whenever neck injury is suspected, including flexion and extension injuries. (Ludwig ch 6)

Heart: what was missed

An acute aortic dissection is expected to extend on both sides of the diaphragm. Even so, the heart is cut out on its own and the aorta trimmed to 2 cm for weighing. What has been lost?

Answer: The continuity of the dissection across the diaphragmatic plane. The source makes two separate recommendations, and it helps to keep them apart. Chapter 1 says that when a lesion involves or crosses the diaphragm, as an acute aortic dissection does, en masse removal (Letulle) worked from the back of the organ mass gives the best result. Chapter 3 says an ascending aortic dissection is a reason to take the heart out en bloc with the chest organs. Either way, the heart shouldn’t have come out alone. (Ludwig ch 1 (Letulle, diaphragmatic plane) and ch 3 (en bloc for ascending dissection))

Pancreas: which cut

The pancreatic duct is dilated. Which plane do you cut in?

Answer: Parallel sagittal sections. The frontal plane is the routine because it shows the most tissue. When the duct is dilated, though, parallel sagittal sections are preferred, because they follow the duct instead of crossing it. (Ludwig ch 5)

What the source does not cover

  • The layered neck dissection itself. Ludwig gives the principle: extensive neck incisions are allowed in medicolegal cases, and the brain comes out first. For the technique, he points to Vanezis and to Adams.
  • Indian medicolegal procedure. This is a Mayo Clinic hospital manual, so it doesn’t cover inquest requisition, the role of the magistrate, or preserving viscera the Indian way. Reddy and Vij cover those.
  • The infant lung flotation test and other stillbirth questions.
  • The adrenals as a separate dissection. They appear in the weight tables at 11.5 g combined (range 8.3 to 16.7 g), but no technique is given.
  • Autopsies in special types of death: hanging, drowning, burns, firearm injuries, and decomposed or exhumed bodies.
  • The source’s spleen and brain weight tables are divided by race as well as age, based on American studies from 1974 and 1949. Only the age pattern is carried here. The racial breakdown was left out on purpose. It’s old, it comes from a different population, and presenting it as a norm for Indian practice would mislead rather than inform.
  • Heart weight appears here only as a reference point at 70 kg. The source has a full table by body weight from 30 kg upward, and that table is what you should check for any real case.
